# Finance — Banking & Treasury

Job-work pricing, costing and customer credit are in [[Pricing]]. This file is the company's own money: which bank it sits in, and what is done with the surplus.

## Banking
- The company banks with **Central Bank of India (CBI)** — the current account everything runs out of.
- An **ICICI relationship manager** came to the office and pitched moving the factory's whole banking infrastructure from CBI to ICICI, walking through the benefits of the switch (see [[Day12]]).
- Day-to-day money movement and outstanding balances are tracked in **Miracle Software** by uncle, audited by **Shailesh Bhai** every 4–5 months (see [[Business]], Leadership and [[Pricing]]).

## FD / OD — freeing up cash without breaking the deposit
- Surplus sits in **fixed deposits (FD)**. To use that money without touching the deposit, the bank gives an **overdraft (OD)** against it: the OD is a loan sitting on top of the FD, drawn as needed and charged interest on what is drawn. Once the money comes back, the OD balance is cleared.
- Why this rather than just withdrawing the FD: breaking a deposit early forfeits the accrued interest, takes a premature-withdrawal penalty, and resets the rate. An OD leaves the deposit running at its contracted rate and charges interest only on the amount actually drawn, for the days it is out. For money that leaves and returns inside a couple of weeks — which is exactly the IPO cycle below — the OD interest is a fraction of the interest given up by breaking the FD.

## Loan against property — the second funding route for IPOs
- A **home loan has been applied for from ICICI**, mortgaged against **uncle's house**. It is not for a house: the money is drawn to fund IPO applications.
- **It behaves like the FD/OD arrangement above** — a sanctioned limit rather than a lump sum. Interest is payable **only on the amount actually drawn**, at **7%**. Draw nothing, owe nothing.
- The cycle: if an IPO is allotted, the money is drawn, the shares are sold and the loan is **repaid immediately**. If nothing is allotted, no money was ever drawn, so there is nothing to repay.
- **The arithmetic that makes it work:** a large allotment returns more than the 7% cost of the money. The spread between listing gain and 7% is the whole profit, and it is only a profit if the return clears 7% *and* the allotment is large enough for the gain to matter against the interest on the full drawn amount.
- **Sizing:** the loan is against the house's value — a house valued at ~₹1.2 crore supports a loan of roughly **₹80 lakh**, about two-thirds of value.
- **The structural point (see [[Day14]]):** none of this machinery works without the house underneath it. The asset is what converts into borrowing capacity, and the borrowing capacity is what buys the IPO applications. Money makes money — but only on top of an asset that was already owned.
- This way is risky only because the FD. 

## IPO applications
- An IPO application is tied to a **PAN**, and one PAN can carry only one application in an issue. So the number of applications the family can put into a single IPO is the number of PANs it controls, and each application is an independent ticket in the allotment lottery.
- **A HUF holds a PAN separate from the individual's.** "Ruchit" and "Ruchit HUF" are two distinct applicants and both can apply to the same IPO.
- Mechanics: money is transferred from the **company's CBI account** into each individual and HUF account, each account is left holding enough balance to cover its own application, the **forms are printed**, and they are **physically submitted at the bank** (see [[Day12]]).
- **HNI applications cannot be filed online** through a broker like Zerodha, which is why it runs on printed forms over the bank counter. The cash for them is raised through the FD/OD route above.
- Why the physical form: broker/UPI applications are capped at ₹5 lakh per application, so anything larger has to go through the bank's own ASBA route — net banking or a form at the branch — and HNI-sized applications sit above that cap.

## Extra PANs via company registration (market practice, not ours)
- The ICICI relationship manager also described a loophole people use to get past the one-PAN-one-application limit: **registering combinations of people as companies**, since each registered entity receives its own PAN, then applying to the same IPO from every one of those PANs. It scales the number of applications far past what a family's individual and HUF PANs allow.
- Recorded as how the market behaves. The factory's own practice is the individual/HUF route above.
