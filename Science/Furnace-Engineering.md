## Heating: 3-phase resistance circuit
- Each furnace runs on all 3 phases. Each phase feeds 2 power inlets, split across **2 zones** (top and bottom): each zone gets all 3 phases via 3 heating elements (one heating element per phase).
- A heating element here is physically a **resistive coil made of Kanthal** — the FeCrAl (iron-chromium-aluminium) wire is wound into a coil shape to fit a long resistance length into a compact space, the same way any electric heating coil is built. So the element genuinely is coil-shaped; the terminology rule is about which noun to use in writing, not a physical distinction. Standard up to ~1400°C. Why FeCrAl over cheaper nichrome: it grows a protective aluminium-oxide skin at high temp that stops it burning away, giving a higher max temperature and longer element life.
- **Kanthal vs Nichrome**: Nichrome has lower electrical resistance and heats up faster, which is why it's the standard choice for quick-response, lower-temperature commercial appliances (e.g. toasters) — but it doesn't hold up at the sustained high temperatures a heat-treatment furnace runs at the way Kanthal's oxide layer does. Different tool for a different job: Nichrome optimizes for fast response at moderate temperature, Kanthal optimizes for surviving sustained high temperature.
- Terminology note: always call this a **"heating element,"** not a bare "coil" — in this factory "coil" is also the word for a steel raw-material batch (a mill coil/rolling), and the two meanings collide constantly since both live in the same sentence-space ("furnace," "zone," "phase"). "Heating element" is unambiguous either way.
- Why elements matter operationally: they're a consumable. They thin over years (oxide spalling), resistance rises, power delivered drops → furnace heats slower and may stop reaching setpoint → soak times silently stretch → throughput and quality drift. A sagged element touching the load shorts the circuit — dead furnace mid-cycle with a customer lot inside. Elements have lead time; spares on shelf matter.
- ⚠️ **Safety concern:** hot and cold steel look identical below ~500°C — a part at 400°C looks room-temperature and takes skin off on contact. Never touch metal near/in a furnace without checking (spit-drop, IR thermometer, or ask). Cotton clothing only near furnaces — synthetic fabrics melt into skin.

## Neutral/earth removed from the 3-phase heating circuit
- Currently the 3-phase heater wiring has **no neutral or earth connection**; earlier it did. Stated reason: risk of "back power."
- ⚠️ **Safety concern — flagged for expert review, don't take at face value.** Removing earth/ground is normally a step *away* from safety, not toward it — earthing is what gives a fault current somewhere to go other than through a person. "Back power" most plausibly means a floating/ungrounded delta heating circuit was seeing induced or fault current flow back through an earth path when one existed (e.g. an imbalance or a wiring fault upstream), and the shop's fix was to remove the ground path rather than find and fix the source of the stray current. That may solve the symptom while leaving a real fault uncorrected and removing a layer of shock protection. Get an outside/qualified electrician's opinion before treating this as settled practice.
- ⚠️ **Safety concern (general electrical/fire):** never use water on an oil or electrical fire — on oil it causes a steam explosion that sprays burning oil; CO2, dry powder, or foam only. This factory doesn't quench (no quench-oil tanks), but it does have live electrical panels and an LPG burn-off flame (below), so the rule still applies.

## Contactors vs thyristors (power control)
- **Contactor:** an electromechanical switch — when the controller calls for more heat, the contactor closes and delivers full (100%) power in discrete on/off bursts. Visible on a temperature graph as a stepped/sawtooth pattern rather than a smooth curve. This is **on/off (bang-bang) control.**
- **Thyristor (SCR):** a solid-state switch that can deliver a controlled *percentage* of power smoothly, not just 100/0. Gives finer temperature control and puts less thermal/electrical shock on the heating elements each cycle, extending element life.
- **Why contactors fail:** every switching cycle draws an arc across the contacts as they open/close. Over thousands of cycles this arcing pits and erodes the contact surface; contacts can weld shut (stuck ON → runaway overheating) or fail to make contact (stuck OFF → no heating, cycle fails silently). This is a known, expected failure mode of contactor-based control, not a one-off fault — it is the direct mechanical price paid for the simpler/cheaper switching technology versus a thyristor.
- Engineering implication: contactor failures are a recurring maintenance item, not an anomaly to chase down each time — worth a scheduled contact-inspection/replacement interval, and worth costing a thyristor upgrade against the accumulated cost of stuck-contactor incidents (scrapped/re-run lots, downtime, and the stuck-ON case is also a runaway-overheat safety concern, not just a quality one).

## Temperature control architecture
- Control is done via **PID (Proportional-Integral-Derivative) logic running on PLC controllers**. PID compares the actual reading to the setpoint and computes an output from three terms: Proportional (reacts to how far off the current reading is right now), Integral (corrects a persistent small offset that P alone leaves behind — accumulated error over time), Derivative (reacts to how fast the error is changing, to stop the temperature overshooting past setpoint before it settles). This is the standard algorithm behind virtually all industrial temperature control, not something specific to this factory.
- **Why this matters given our contactors (see below), not thyristors:** a PID controller's raw output is a continuous value (e.g. "62% power"), but a contactor can only be fully on or fully off — it cannot deliver 62% directly. So the PLC has to convert that continuous PID output into **time-proportioning**: switching the contactor on and off within a fixed short window so that, on average, it's on for roughly the commanded percentage of the time (e.g. "on for ~6 seconds out of every 10"). This is exactly why the temperature graph shows discrete on/off bursts rather than a smooth line even though the underlying control logic (PID) is continuous — and it's also *why* contactors take so many switching cycles and wear out the way described below. A thyristor wouldn't need this workaround since it can output a true continuous percentage directly.
- Terminology: a **thermocouple** is just the sensing element — it measures temperature and nothing else. A **thermostat** is the control unit that takes that reading and acts on it (running the PID logic above and opening/closing the contactors accordingly).
- All 4 big furnaces (Bhaktinagar 11/12 and Vavdi 9/10) have **4 thermocouples** each, numbered 1–4 from top to bottom — 2 per zone — but only **2 thermostats**: one per zone, each independently driving its own zone's contactors/heating elements off its zone's sensing.
- One thermostat is **master**, the other **slave**. This is *setpoint coordination*, not consolidation — each zone's thermostat still controls its own heating elements from its own local reading, but the slave follows the master's setpoint logic rather than hunting for setpoint on its own. The placement is furnace-dependent, but generally the **master sits at the lower zone's upper end (thermocouple 3)** and the **slave at the upper zone's lower end (thermocouple 2)**.
- Why independent heating per zone, but coordinated via master/slave: a furnace is never uniform at a single point — top and bottom genuinely differ, so each zone needs its own sensing and heating to correct for it. But without coordination the two loops could drift apart or fight each other; master/slave keeps the whole furnace acting as one coordinated unit.
- Thermocouple aging/drift risk still applies here regardless of how many sensors exist: a drifted master sensor drives the whole furnace off-spec while looking normal on the display.
- A front-desk display (where uncle sits) shows live temperature for furnaces 11 and 12 as **raw numeric values, not a graph**: 16 channels total, with **2 thermocouples per furnace** in use (one per zone — the furnace's own panel shows all 4; sending only 2 to the office is deliberate, to avoid over-engineering), the remaining channels unused/reading zero — bought with more capacity than currently needed. The office displays at both sites work this way (wiring of the office feed: see Thermocouple panel wiring & verification below).
- This same temperature data is also logged into software called **ESCAN**, which stores it in a simple local (SQLite) database and is what actually **renders it as a graph/chart** — the front-desk display itself never shows a graph, only live numbers per channel. Temperature is therefore double-recorded: automatically via ESCAN, and manually by workers on paper log sheets as a backup/cross-check — the paper log is also where gas consumption gets recorded (see Gas consumption measurement below), since that side has no automated equivalent to ESCAN.
- The manual paper log is prone to ordinary human error (a worker reading/writing a value wrong). But the automated path isn't perfectly clean either — the data transfer from thermostat to ESCAN's database itself occasionally faults. That's why Ruchit wrote a Python script to work with ESCAN's underlying SQLite database directly, rather than relying solely on ESCAN's own interface — a direct fix for the automated side's own data-integrity gaps.
- Why clear worker handwriting on the log sheets matters operationally: it's the fast debugging path — when a part comes back soft, decarbed, or otherwise off, the log sheet (cross-checked against ESCAN for temperature) is how the cause gets traced back to gas usage or a temperature cycle deviation.

### Data link: display → CPU (how ESCAN actually receives data)
- The physical link carrying data from the digital display into the computer's CPU for **ESCAN** is a serial cable — **RS232 or RS485**.
- The chain is one-directional end to end: the furnace's **thermocouple is a pure emitter** (it generates a voltage from temperature and can't receive anything), the display reads it, and the display's cable pushes that data to the CPU. The CPU never sends anything back down the line. So this link is **simplex**, not half or full duplex.
- Communication modes, for reference:
  - **Simplex** — data flows only A → B; B cannot transmit. Like a keyboard to a CPU: the CPU never talks back to the keyboard.
  - **Half duplex** — both A and B can transmit, but only one at a time. Like a walkie-talkie.
  - **Full duplex** — both transmit simultaneously. Like a modern phone call.
- Why the distinction is practically relevant here: a simplex link means the CPU has **no way to acknowledge, request a retry, or poll for a missed reading** — if a value is corrupted or dropped in transit, nothing in the protocol notices. That is a plausible mechanism behind the known thermostat→ESCAN data-transfer faults noted under Temperature control architecture above, and it's why the fix had to be at the database end (Ruchit's Python script) rather than at the link.
- RS232 vs RS485 also matters for a shop floor: RS232 is single-ended, short-range, and point-to-point; RS485 is differential and rejects electrical noise far better over long cable runs — which is the relevant property in a shed full of contactors switching 3-phase heating loads.

The gas-controller idea above is deliberately smaller in scope: just the one missing sensor/logging piece, not a wholesale replacement of the existing setup.

The tradeoff: the pot itself adds thermal mass that needs heating, raising the annealing cost — which directly erodes the factory's core cost advantage of running single-pot instead of the industry-standard two-pot/bell-pot setup (see [[Process]], the single-pot cost edge). Classic precision-vs-cost call, and the factory has so far chosen cost.

## Thermocouple panel wiring & verification
- All 4 big furnaces carry **4 thermocouples each**, numbered **1 to 4 from top to bottom** — 2 per zone.
- Each thermocouple has a pair of wires: **white = positive, blue = negative**. The pair is always handled together for any connection.
- The pairs run from the furnace into that furnace's panel, labeled either furnace-prefixed (**FC9-1 … FC9-4**) or simply **1–4** (unambiguous, since one panel serves one furnace).
- Inside the panel, the furnace wire connects to a second wire running to the panel's display — a simple **2-way junction**. A thermocouple whose reading is also sent to the office gets a third pair wired in **parallel** — a **3-way junction** — running to the office.
- At the office end the wires are labeled **furnace–channel** (e.g. FC10-1, FC9-2) and land on the office display's screw-pin terminals (the 16-channel display — see Temperature control architecture).
- Which thermocouples get passed to the office is the factory's own choice. The convention (not strictly followed): pass the ones **not** close to a thermostat, since the thermostat-adjacent readings are already being acted on locally.

### Verification procedure (calibrator)
- ⚠️ Done only with the furnace **not operating and main power supply OFF** — with the supply dead, nothing in the panel can generate electricity, which is what makes it safe to work bare-handed in the wiring. All thermocouples then read room temperature, which is exactly what makes the check possible.
- Tool: the **calibrator** — a handheld device that can both measure and generate small millivolt signals; runs on a 9V battery (kept with it on the small workshop table — see [[Workers]], Small daily supplies). Set to measuring mode for this job.
- Work one furnace at a time, thermocouple 1 to 4, top to bottom. For each: connect the furnace-side pair, confirm the label really points at that thermocouple, and read the calibrator:
  - **~30–35** → reading room temperature, wiring correct.
  - **~40–50** → polarity reversed. A reversed thermocouple doesn't show a negative value — it shows a wrong-looking voltage instead.
  - **"1" / OPEN** → open circuit: the wires aren't properly connected to the furnace's thermocouple.
- Once the furnace-side pair checks clean, connect it to the panel display pin, plus the third parallel pair if that thermocouple feeds the office; then re-check with the calibrator at the office end before landing the wires on the office display pins.
- This is a **two-person job**: a worker stationed at the furnace (reaching the bottom and various positions on the furnace body) while the other person checks at the panel.

## Unloading and loading (the full changeover)

This routine is **essentially the same for every coil**, whatever the material — only the heat-treatment cycle that follows it differs by material (and those cycles are already pre-determined for most common materials; see [[Process]]). Parts and samples can differ from the coil routine.

**The lid itself** is lifted by 3 hooks on its top, the lifting chains forming a cone up to the crane hook. The 3 hooks are near-equidistant around the lid — that's what keeps the lid stable and level in the air: a 3-point equidistant suspension holds the lid's centre of gravity directly under the crane hook.

**Why the whole thing is done fast:** the next batch is started as soon as the previous one finishes wherever possible, so the furnace is still hot and less energy is needed to bring it back to temperature — a direct reduction in the electricity bill. Every minute the lid sits open is heat leaving.

### 0) Pre-loading
The next coil is made ready *before* the furnace is opened — picked up by crane from its wires and set onto the coil holder — so that once the lid is off, the swap happens immediately rather than with a hot furnace standing open waiting.

### 1) Unloading (done as the cycle finishes, around 500–600°C)
1. Open the **wheels** — the big screws clamping the furnace lid.
2. Open the **main furnace vacuum valve**: a plain ball valve sitting on top of the lid, the biggest valve in the factory, and what holds the vacuum. Outside air rushes in and **the pressure reading jumps from negative to 0 instantly**.
3. Lift the lid off with the crane and set it aside. The furnace core is now open — parts of it still glowing red, with the material inside.
4. Lift the **coil holder** out with the crane and set it well away from the working area to cool in open air. This is the hardest step: the crane hooks have to be attached to the holder while it is inside a furnace that is still hot.

⚠️ Safety concern: steps 3 and 4 are done at 500–600°C with an open furnace and a hot load swinging on a crane, under deliberate time pressure to save heat. That combination — heat, overhead load, and haste — is where the serious injury risk in this factory sits.

### 2) Loading
1. Lift the coil onto the holder and place the holder inside the furnace.
2. Lift the lid and set it on top of the furnace.
3. **Tighten the screw wheels moderately — NOT fully tight.** Why: the vacuum then pulls the lid's inner rubber seal inward, and the seal needs room to draw in and seat itself. Clamping fully first prevents the rubber from settling into place, so the seal is worse, not better.
4. Attach the equipment wiring, on the furnaces that have it (9 and 12): oxygen meter and solenoid valve.
5. Join the vacuum pump onto the furnace valve and open the valve. The vacuum pump switch is on the panel itself.
6. **Start the pump.** Furnace is around 200–300°C at this point, and this stage acts as a **pre-bake**: it pulls out the ordinary air that came in with the lid open, impurities off the coil, and — importantly — **water vapour from moisture on the coil**, which arrived from the outside atmosphere. Doing this while the chamber is already warm is what drives the moisture off rather than leaving it to boil out later into the process atmosphere. In practice the wait happens naturally during steps 3 and 4.
7. **Now** tighten the screws fully — after the seal has drawn in.
8. Start raising temperature again on the PLC, from the current ~200–300°C.
9. The pump reaches only about **-0.5 kgf/cm²**. It is old and worn; it used to pull much more. See the note below.
10. Once that pressure is reached, lock the valve and let temperature rise.
11. At around **500°C, apply the vacuum again.** The process proper starts here.

- **Degraded vacuum pumps** — achievable vacuum has fallen to ~-0.5 kgf/cm² through wear and tear, down from significantly better when new. **Both sites' pumps are equally worn**, so pump condition is not a difference between Vavdi and Bhaktinagar and has been ruled out as the driver of the decarb inconsistency (see Decarb — atmosphere control below).

Water-jacket inlet/outlet pipes are fixed onto the lid as part of closing up (see Water cooling circuit below).

## Water cooling circuit
- Purpose: keep door seals, fans, bearings, and (on any vacuum-type unit) the shell cool while the furnace interior runs 850°C+; rubber seals and motor bearings degrade fast if hot.
- One furnace: water is routed below it and discharged into an open metal tub, then recycled.
- Both furnaces route their cooling water through an **open, visible path on purpose** — not routed through a closed/hidden pipe — specifically so an operator can visually confirm water is still flowing just by looking. This is a low-cost substitute for a proper flow sensor/alarm: it works only if someone actually looks, and gives no warning at night or during an unattended stretch. A flow switch + buzzer (a few hundred rupees of hardware) would remove the "someone has to notice" dependency. ⚠️ Safety concern: cooling failure risks seal/bearing damage and, on gas-fired units, potential leaks.

## Pressure measurement
- **Coverage:** the old **analog pressure gauge is on every furnace, including furnace 8**. The **digital** gauge exists only on **furnaces 9 and 12** — the same two that carry the digital solenoid, and the two the single oxygen probe moves between.
- **Every pressure reading here, analog or digital, is gauge (relative) pressure, not absolute** — it reads relative to the surrounding atmospheric pressure, which is why vacuum shows as a negative number (e.g. -0.5 kgf/cm²) rather than as a small positive absolute value.
- Why relative is the right choice and not a shortcut: what matters operationally is the *difference* between inside and outside the furnace — that difference is what drives leakage in or out, what holds the lid seal, and what says whether atmosphere is being kept in or air pulled in. A true absolute reference would also be measuring against a perfect vacuum, and **a perfect 0 vacuum is practically impossible to reach**, so the absolute number would carry no extra usable information.

## Gas lines: nitrogen, LPG, and the atmosphere system
- Nitrogen and LPG currently run through **one common sideline near the furnace**. ⚠️ **Safety concern:** these should be kept apart and the furnace isolated from both.
  - LPG is flammable and heavier than air — leaks pool at floor level and travel until they find an ignition source, and a furnace shed is full of them.
  - N2 is the quiet killer of the heat treatment industry: odorless, doesn't burn, displaces oxygen. A leak in a corner, pit, or someone leaning into a vessel = unconsciousness in seconds, with zero warning (no breathlessness first). More HT workers die from N2 asphyxiation than burns. Never enter or lean into any furnace, pit, or tank that has held N2 without it being ventilated and someone standing outside.
  - Correct practice: cylinders/manifolds outside or at the wall farthest from furnaces, LPG and N2 separated, ventilated, shutoffs reachable without walking past the furnace (in a fire the gas must be cuttable without approaching the fire).
- A broken pressure gauge was in use on an LPG-related line. ⚠️ A broken gauge still installed is worse than none — people glance at it by habit and read garbage as truth, and a wrong LPG pressure reading is a fire/explosion input.
- LPG leak response: no switches on OR off (both spark), shut cylinder valve, ventilate, find leak with soap water — never a flame.
- Workers are made to write "LPG" and "FURNACE" (labels/log on equipment) so they stay aware while working. Labelling is passive; the active version is a printed purge/light-up checklist at each gas-fired unit — lighting a furnace with unburnt gas accumulated inside is the classic small-shop explosion.

### Gas distribution topology (bottle → furnace)
- Originally **one flow meter set per site**, shared: one at Bhaktinagar between furnaces 11 and 12, one at Vavdi between furnaces 9 and 10 — meaning **one furnace at a time on the meter**.
- **Bhaktinagar now has 2 sets** (LPG + N2 flow meters each), one per furnace, so **11 and 12 can both be metered independently**. This removes the metering limitation there — note it was never a *production* limitation: both furnaces at a site always ran simultaneously and gas inlet to both was always physically possible, the sharing only meant you couldn't get proper individual numbers off both at once. Vavdi is still on the shared single set.
- Why the second set matters for the decarb hunt: per-furnace gas figures are what let you compare furnace 11 against furnace 12 directly, instead of attributing a shared meter's total to whichever furnace was on it.
- The shared vacuum pump is likewise not a bottleneck: vacuum is only drawn at the **start** of a cycle, occupying at most about half an hour, after which the furnace is locked and holds its own vacuum — freeing the pump for the other furnace for the rest of the cycle.
- **Furnace 8 bypasses this entirely** — it takes gas direct from the bottle, with no flow meter in between, so it falls back on the older weighing method below.
- Each flow meter carries **separate flow paths for LPG and for nitrogen**, so the two gases are metered individually.
- **After the flow meter the two gases merge into one unified gas pipe**, which runs to whichever furnace is selected. So the mixing is deliberate and happens downstream of metering — the meters see pure single gases, the furnace receives the blend. (This is also the physical reality behind the shared-sideline safety concern above: LPG and N2 genuinely share one pipe by design from the meter onward.)
- The unified pipe **enters the furnace at the BOTTOM**, through an inlet fitted with a **spring check NRV**.

### Spring check NRV (non-return valve)
- A purely **mechanical, one-way valve** — no electrics, no control signal. Gas must go into the furnace only, never back out toward the meter and bottles.
- **How it works:** an internal spring holds a sealing sheet/disc against a seat. Forward gas pressure pushes the sheet off its seat and flows through; the moment forward pressure drops, the spring snaps the sheet back onto the seat and blocks reverse flow. The blocking is automatic and instant — it needs nothing to detect the reversal, because the loss of pressure *is* the trigger.
- Why it matters that it's the last thing before the furnace: it's what stops hot furnace atmosphere, and the pressure inside the chamber, from pushing back up the gas line toward the LPG and N2 bottles.
- **NRVs also sit inside the flow meters**, not just at the furnace inlet.
- **Convention now in force:** every NRV has its **flow direction marked with a white-marker arrow drawn on its body** after servicing, so the correct orientation is readable at a glance on reassembly and no one has to re-derive it. (Same principle as labelling furnace numbers on the thermocouple wires in the panel — see Thermocouple panel wiring & verification.)
- ⚠️ Safety concern: an NRV fitted backwards or with a failed seat on an LPG line is a reverse-flow path toward the cylinders. The arrow marking is what makes an incorrect refit visible before the furnace runs.

### Decarb — atmosphere control & troubleshooting
- Why furnaces run nitrogen: N2 is inert — flooding the chamber removes the oxygen that steals surface carbon (see [[Metallurgy]] for the underlying chemistry). LPG can also serve as a protective/carbon-rich atmosphere source.
- Current reality: this is being tuned somewhat by brute force. Decarb readings are inconsistent across nominally similar cycles — sometimes ~0%, sometimes as high as 241 microns (see the Power-client incident in [[Day3]]) — and the root cause isn't understood yet. Candidate variables under suspicion: insufficient LPG in the atmosphere, or a leak somewhere in the gas/atmosphere system.
- Why it's genuinely this hard to pin down: the furnace atmosphere isn't just C/CO/CO2/LPG in a clean reaction. Heating elements sit exposed directly to the furnace atmosphere (not shielded — see the bell pot idea below), so the Kanthal itself can react with the atmosphere; the Cera Wool insulation may also trap some gases. With that many simultaneous side reactions, exact modeling isn't practical — the factory can only realistically brute-force the most important gases/parameters in the controller rather than account for everything happening inside.
- An outside consultant (found via LinkedIn, experienced with furnace work) suggested a specific candidate cause: heating-element wires touching each other, which could block LPG from reaching some parts of the furnace — worth checking as a physical/mechanical cause distinct from the atmosphere-chemistry theories above (see [[Day4]]).
- Comparative clue: the two Vavdi furnaces have far less decarb trouble than the Bhaktinagar ones, despite all four sharing the same design — so whatever drives the decarb inconsistency lives in something that differs between the two sites, not in the furnace design itself. A standing comparison axis for the root-cause hunt (see [[Day5]]).
- The actual instrumentation gap behind the guesswork: gas consumption (LPG/N2) has no digital chart the way temperature does via ESCAN — it's only ever logged manually on paper (see Gas consumption measurement below), so there's no way to overlay a gas-usage graph against the ESCAN temperature graph to spot correlations. See Ideas (not yet in practice) above for the proposed fix (digitizing gas consumption into a chart alongside ESCAN's temperature chart).

### Oxygen probe / Carbon Potential (CP) control
- The factory owns **exactly one oxygen probe unit** — probe + display controller, bought as a package from a dealer in Vadodara, roughly **₹1 lakh**. "Oxymeter" and "oxygen probe" here mean this same single instrument. Cost is why there is only one. It has been made to work successfully.
- **It moves between furnaces.** It was originally on **furnace 12 (Bhaktinagar)** and is **currently on furnace 9 (Vavdi)** — the unit on furnace 9 is that same relocated probe, not a second one. So at any given time exactly one of the four big furnaces has CP instrumentation and the other three are run without it.
- The consequence worth being explicit about: the three uninstrumented furnaces are still run on judgement for atmosphere, and a probe reading taken on one furnace does not transfer to another — this is the sharpest instrumentation gap behind the decarb work below.
- It's not a plain oxygen percentage meter. It measures oxygen, but from that reading it also derives **Carbon Potential (CP)** — see [[Metallurgy]] for what CP means metallurgically. Internally the probe still only measures oxygen; CP is calculated from that oxygen reading via chemical equilibrium, not measured directly.
- **Sensor technology**: the probe uses a **zirconium dioxide (ZrO2)** element, which only becomes electrically active above ~600°C — that's why the display shows "OPEN" instead of a mV reading below that temperature.
- **Display behavior, in sequence as the furnace heats up**:
  - Probe wired but sitting in open air at room temperature: displays "OPEN."
  - At ~600°C, the display starts reading around **-20mV**, meaning the atmosphere's oxygen level is still effectively the same as room air.
  - As oxygen is driven out toward a full vacuum, the reading approaches **0mV**.
  - Beyond that, readings climb up to roughly **1150mV**.
  - Past roughly **1000mV**, CP starts climbing into positive territory — meaning the atmosphere now has surplus free carbon, which starts depositing onto the steel surface rather than stripping from it (visibly so: black carbon sits on top of the coil, enough to wipe off on a finger). This is **surface deposition only — the carbon does not fuse into the steel's interior**. Fusing carbon inward is **carburizing**, a distinct process needing higher temperatures than these cycles reach (see [[Metallurgy]], Carbon Potential/Carburizing).
- **The science behind the reading**: LPG cracks (breaks down) into a specific mix of CO and CO2. The probe reads the oxygen level (in millivolts) produced by that specific gas mix. From that oxygen reading, the controller calculates — via chemical equilibrium — how much free carbon that gas mix will force into (or pull out of) the steel surface. So the whole CP measurement is an oxygen reading interpreted through known chemistry, not a direct carbon sensor.
- **Recorded observation on furnace 9**: as a cycle starts, the mV reading falls steadily — 11, 10, 9, 8, 7 … down to 0mV — and then jumps straight to about **1100mV** in one step, with no intermediate values. The falling part matches the expected behaviour above (oxygen being driven out toward 0mV). The step to 1100mV differs from the documented sequence, which is a progressive climb from 0mV up through the 1000mV+ range.
- Likely sourcing note: the Vadodara dealer who sold this probe probably isn't the manufacturer — probably sources the units from China and resells with service, at a large margin (unconfirmed, see [[Day4]]).

### Gas consumption measurement (pressure injection)
- Neither LPG nor N2 usage is measured with a dedicated flow meter. Instead, consumption is inferred from the **pressure of the gas injected** while heat-treating: since gas is injected at a known/monitored pressure, tracking how that pressure behaves over a cycle lets the factory back-calculate the ingestion rate — effectively using pressure as a proxy for flow rather than metering flow directly. This is a lower-cost substitute for inline flow meters on both the LPG and nitrogen lines.
- **Before any of this monitoring existed**, gas consumption was worked out entirely by hand: estimation, plus **weighing the cylinders after each cycle** and taking the drop in weight as the gas consumed. Crude but sound in principle — mass is mass — and it remains the fallback for anything not on a meter, including furnace 8.
- This pressure/gas-influx reading is recorded manually by workers on paper log sheets, not by any automated system — unlike temperature (see ESCAN below), there's no digital logging for gas consumption, which makes it the more error-prone of the two records.
- Why it matters beyond engineering: this is the actual number both gas cost accounting and supplier negotiation are built on — see [[Business]] (supplier negotiation) and [[Pricing]] (cost formula).
- **Upgrade now installed:** N2 and LPG **flow meters are in place at both factories**, connected to the N2 and LPG bottles kept there, reading in **litres per minute**. This replaces the pressure-as-proxy-for-flow inference above with a direct flow measurement — pressure told you how hard the gas was being pushed, litres/minute tells you how much gas actually went in, which is the number both the decarb investigation and gas cost accounting always wanted.

## LPG relief and burn-off system
- A **solenoid-operated pressure relief valve** attached to the furnace's pressure valve: if pressure rises too high, it vents rather than letting pressure build toward a rupture/leak point elsewhere. Solenoid (electrically actuated) construction means the release can also be controlled/triggered electrically, not just by a fixed mechanical set-point. This is a genuine safety improvement over an unmanaged vent.
- Coverage: the digital solenoid is fitted on **furnace 9 (Vavdi) and furnace 12 (Bhaktinagar)** only — the same two furnaces the single oxygen probe moves between. Unlike the probe, there are two solenoids and they stay fitted. Furnaces 10 and 11 have neither. ⚠️ Safety concern: two of the four big furnaces run their LPG relief without the automatic trigger, so on those the manual/mechanical layer is the only layer.
- It's a **trigger mechanism**: the valve opens once the measured pressure crosses a set threshold. The digital valve display shows both the current live value and the target/threshold value, so it's clear at a glance how close the system is to tripping.
- The old **analog pressure dial sits directly above/inline with the new digital valve**, so there's always a rough analog backup reading (in kgf/cm²) alongside the precise digital one — if the digital electronics ever fail, the analog gauge still shows roughly what's going on.
- An **extra manual valve sits upstream of the solenoid valve**, purely as an additional layer of safety redundancy — even if the automatic system fails, there's a manual cutoff before it.
- Deliberately positioned using an existing outlet, but kept physically far from the hooks used to lift the furnace lid — sound practice: keeps a gas outlet away from a high-traffic mechanical-interference point (a swinging lid hook near a gas fitting is a needless snag/impact risk).
- Vented gas isn't just released to open air — it's routed to a **standing burn-off flame** (a small pilot flame burns the vented LPG continuously, lit with a lighter), wrapped in the same silica insulation (specifically **Cera Wool**, a ceramic/silica fiber insulation) used inside the furnace to stop heat building up around the burner housing. Why burn it off instead of venting raw: an open LPG vent creates an invisible, spreading flammable cloud looking for an ignition source; a controlled pilot flame instead burns the gas immediately and completely at a known, fixed point — converting an uncontrolled hazard into a controlled, visible one. Same principle as a flare stack in larger industrial plants, at small-shop scale.
